      How to play

      Play in threes. Each round, one person brings the news, one plays a character and one referees. Swap after every round so everyone plays all three roles.

      The roles

      Newsbearer

      Draw a news story as your prompt. Lead with something personal, never a generalisation like "I care about climate change". Talk to the character in a personal, relatable way, using the three steps below. You can stick to your own personal story, or take the extra challenge of weaving the news in. You win the round if the character says yes to your one thing and you get no red card.

      Character

      Draw a character and read out the front of the card: name, age, job and scores. Show the triggers and door openers to the referee only. Start the round with your opening line, then stay in role: open up when the newsbearer finds a door opener and push back when they hit a trigger.

      Referee

      Read the character's triggers and door openers. Run the five-minute clock, hold up cards when the newsbearer slips, and call the verdict at the end.

      The story and the character are drawn separately, at random.

      The conversation, in order

      1. My why

        Open with why you care, through a moment from your own life: a place, a person, a smell, a feeling. If you are using the news story, connect the two. A few sentences is enough.

        Avoid general openers such as "I care about climate change", "the planet is in trouble" or "we all need to do our bit". They sound like everyone else and cost a point.

        Based on Simon Sinek, Start with Why

      2. Your world

        Ask about the character's life and listen for what they feel and what they need. Show them you heard it.

        Based on Marshall Rosenberg, Nonviolent Communication

      3. One thing

        Ask for one action. It has to pass the detective test.

        Motive
        They care about it, for their own reasons.
        Means
        They have the money, time and skills.
        Opportunity
        They could do it this week.

        Detectives use motive, means and opportunity to work out who could have committed a crime. Here you're planning a small act of climate action, and the character is your accomplice.

      See a worked example: a coffee shop owner
      Story
      Coffee farms are getting weeks of extra harmful heat. Droughts in Brazil and Vietnam pushed coffee prices to record highs.
      Character
      Marco, 44, runs a coffee shop in Glasgow's West End. Scepticism 3, time 1, wallet 2, reach 3.

      Marco

      "Make it quick, I've got a queue out the door."

      1 My why

      Messenger

      "My first love was a barista in Bologna. Every morning that summer he made me a cappuccino with a wee heart on top. I still can't smell fresh coffee without being 19 again. So when I read that the farms growing those beans are baking in the heat, it actually hurt."

      Why it works: it starts with a real moment and a feeling, so Marco hears a person, not a campaign. Compare "Climate change is a huge issue and I really care about the planet", which tells him nothing about you and costs a point.

      2 Your world

      Messenger

      "What's it been like for you this year, with bean prices?"

      Marco

      "Brutal. My roaster's put prices up twice. I've had to charge four pound for a flat white and my regulars moan at me."

      Messenger

      "So you're stuck in the middle. You're fed up because you want to be fair to your regulars and still keep the doors open. Is that right?"

      Marco

      "Aye. That's exactly it."

      Why it works: this is Nonviolent Communication. The messenger names what happened (prices up twice), the feeling (fed up) and the need (fairness, keeping the business going), then checks. Marco feels heard before he hears any news. The referee would tap good question and active listening.

      Messenger

      "I read that the farms in Brazil and Vietnam are getting weeks more of the kind of heat that damages the coffee plants. That's a big part of why your roaster keeps putting prices up."

      Why it works: the news arrives as an answer to his problem, with no numbers and no jargon.

      3 One thing

      Messenger

      "Would you put a wee card by the till this week, saying where your beans come from and why the price went up? Your roaster will have the farm's story already."

      Marco

      "Go on then. Might stop the moaning."

      Motive
      He wants his regulars to understand the price, in his own words.
      Means
      One printed card and ten minutes. No cost.
      Opportunity
      This week, next to the till he stands at all day.

      The referee ticks all six boxes, the character says yes and the screen celebrates.

      Cards

      Yellow card: a warning

      The newsbearer keeps going and drops whatever earned the card.

      • A statistic or number the character didn't ask for
      • Jargon or acronyms, such as net zero, mitigation, decarbonise, carbon footprint, COP or IPCC
      • Talking for 30 seconds without asking a question
      • Telling them what to do: "you should", "you need to"
      • Doom: "it's too late", "we're all doomed"

      Red card: stop and repair

      The newsbearer stops the story and repairs the conversation before going on.

      • A second yellow card
      • Hitting one of the character's triggers
      • Blaming, mocking or shaming the character
      • Arguing with how they feel

      Score

      The referee taps a button every time something happens, on their phone. The page adds up the total.

      Plus one

      • A personal story or feeling
      • A good open question
      • Active listening: naming back a feeling or need
      • Connecting to the character's world

      Minus one

      • Generalising, such as "I care about climate change"
      • Jargon or a banned word
      • A statistic nobody asked for
      • Lecturing, "you should" or doom

      When the referee ticks My why, Your world, One thing, Motive, Means and Opportunity, the screen celebrates. If the character answers maybe or no, or says yes after a red card, the messenger gets shot with a foam dart. Pew pew. Compare scores across rounds in the debrief.

      Repair after a red card

      Say what you think the character feels and needs, then check you got it right.

      "It sounds like you're [feeling] because [need] matters to you. Is that right?"

      Wait for the answer. If they say no, try again. Carry on once they agree you've understood.

      End of the round

      • The character answers: would you do it? Yes, maybe or no. If it isn't a yes, they say which was missing: motive, means or opportunity.
      • The referee names one moment that worked and one to replay.

      Debrief questions

      1. Which one thing would you actually do this week?
      2. What earned a red card, and what repaired it?
      3. Who in your own life is most like your character? What will you say to them now?
